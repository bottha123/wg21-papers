>
> Niccolò Machiavelli, dedicatory letter, [*The Prince*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1232), translated by W. K. Marriott

I wrote this for you. Not for the experts who've sat in these rooms for twenty years. For you, the one who just found out that a group of volunteers decides what goes into C++ and wondered how to join them.

Machiavelli gave a prince the one thing he valued most: what he'd learned watching powerful people up close. This notebook works the same way, smaller and plainer. It's the worn, marked-up notebook I wish someone had pressed into my hands before my first meeting.

The group is real, and you can join it. It's called [WG21](https://isocpp.org/std/the-committee), and it's the committee that owns the C++ standard. Around two hundred people show up to each meeting to argue, vote, and ship a new version of the language every three years.

These volunteers are some of the most careful people you'll ever meet. They'll spend an hour on the placement of a single word, because the stakes are high. You'll come to understand why.

### Who This Notebook Is For

If you're new, this notebook is for you. Maybe you've never been to a meeting, or maybe you've been to a few and still feel lost in the room. Either way, you're in the right place.

You don't need to know every rule in C++. You don't need to have written a compiler. You need only curiosity and the patience to keep showing up.

### What This Notebook Is Not

This isn't a textbook, a manual, or an encyclopedia. It's my notebook, and I take sides in it. I value stability over novelty, and I put the people who use C++ ahead of the people who design it.

When the evidence is thin, I say so. Where the committee has written a rule down, I link to it, so you can check me. Names, fees, and dates are as of 2026, and they'll drift. If a linked rule and I ever disagree, trust the rule.

## 1. The C++ Standard

Start here, because everything else builds on this. The C++ standard is the thing you'll help shape, and it deserves your care.

<div id="in-this-chapter">

- Why standardization is a responsibility, not a game
- The one-sentence oath that fits the whole job
- Why a feature, once standardized, is close to forever, and who pays for it
- Why the standard, the compilers, and the language you use are three different things
- What "the standard" actually is: the working draft, the published standard, technical specifications, and white papers
- How to read the standard without reading all of it
- Who turns the words into compilers, and why that makes them so influential

</div>

### 1.1 Standardization Is a Responsibility

The work you're volunteering for is serious. Banks and hospitals run on C++. When you change the language, you change the ground they stand on.

So this isn't a game. It isn't a place to collect a credential or win an argument. It's a place to leave the language better than you found it.

The urge to get your idea adopted is strong, and it can fool you. Passing a proposal was never the goal. The goal is a better language for the people who use it.

### 1.2 The Delegate's Oath

The duty fits in a single sentence. Some delegates call it the Delegate's Oath, and it goes like this:

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

I vow to do what is best for the language, to make no unnecessary proposals, and to put the needs of sixteen million users ahead of my own.

</div>

Each part guards against a real temptation. "Best for the language" outranks best for you or your employer. It's also the hardest part to live by, because your employer's need is usually a real user's need, dressed in a paycheck.

The trouble starts when your employer asks you for a vote you believe is bad for the language. The oath exists for that moment. Talk the balance through with your manager early, before you're standing in the room with your hand half raised. Principles are cheap until they cost something.

"No unnecessary proposals" puts the burden on you to show a change earns its place.

The last part is the crux of it. Those users never asked you for anything. You serve them first, even when they'll never know your name.

To understand why the oath is this strict, look at what a single feature costs.

### 1.3 Every Feature Is Forever

The hard rule: the standard almost never shrinks. Features get added. They rarely get removed.

C++ got a [regular-expression library](https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/regex) in 2011, and everyone soon learned it was slow. More than a decade later, it's still slow.

It can't be fixed, because a faster version would break programs already built on the old one. So the slow version stays. The mistake is frozen in place.

That's the pattern for everything you will ever add to the standard. Proposing a feature costs you several years. Maintaining it costs everyone else forever.

### 1.4 The People Who Inherit Your Work

The cost is paid by the people who use the language. More than [sixteen million developers](https://www.slashdata.co/post/global-developer-population-trends-2025-how-many-developers-are-there) write C++ today, by one recent estimate. Whatever you standardize, they inherit.

They didn't vote for your feature, and most will never read a proposal. They discover your work as a line in compiler release notes. By then it's permanent.

Users aren't the only ones who pay. Each compiler team must build and maintain every feature, and every textbook, course, and tutorial has to cover it.

<div id="watch-out">

When you catch yourself saying "it's just one small addition," stop. That urge is how careful people do lasting damage. Small is not the same as free. Ask who will maintain it in 2040.

</div>

### 1.5 The Standard Is Not the Language

You might think "C++" and "the C++ standard" are the same thing. They're not. Three different things go by that name.

*The standard* is a document. It's a long, exact text that says what C++ means. It's words, not a program.

A compiler is a program that tries to follow that document. The big compilers don't follow it perfectly, and they don't all agree. So the C++ you can actually use is whatever your compiler accepts.

Keep three things distinct: the standard is the text, the compilers try to follow it, and the living language is what real code relies on.

### 1.6 What "The Standard" Actually Is

The standard comes in a few forms, and the names are worth remembering. Behind every C++ release is a living document called the *working draft*, and it changes all the time. You can [read the current one](https://eel.is/c++draft/) online, and its source lives on [GitHub](https://github.com/cplusplus/draft).

Every three years, the committee freezes the draft and hands it to ISO to publish. That published version is an *International Standard*, or IS. [C++23](https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/23) is one. C++26 [finished its technical work in March 2026](https://herbsutter.com/2026/03/29/c26-is-done-trip-report-march-2026-iso-c-standards-meeting-london-croydon-uk/). Member countries still have to approve the final text before ISO publishes it.

Two lighter documents let people test a feature before it joins the standard. A *Technical Specification*, or TS, is the experimental tryout, and a *white paper* does the same job with far less paperwork. The committee's own [practices guide](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) says the standards bodies have recommended the white paper since 2023.

You may also run into a *Technical Report*, or TR, an older kind that informs rather than tests. You'll rarely meet a new one.

### 1.7 How to Read the Standard

The standard looks scary, and it is dense. But it's organized, and a few habits make it readable. You don't have to read it front to back.

Most of it is *normative text*, the parts that state actual rules. Mixed in are notes and examples, which never add a rule. They only help you understand the rules around them.

Each part has a *stable section label* in brackets, like [\[container.requirements\]](https://eel.is/c++draft/container.requirements). Those labels stay fixed between drafts. Section numbers shift, so point to the label, not the number.

<div id="try-this-today">

Open the working draft and search for a feature you use every day. Find its bracketed label, then read one paragraph of its rules, slowly. That's the kind of text you'd be changing, and sixteen million people stand on it.

</div>

### 1.8 From Standard to Compiler

Publishing the standard doesn't put a feature in your hands. Someone has to build it into a compiler first. Until they do, it stays words on a page.

The bulk of that building falls to *the three implementers*: [GCC](https://gcc.gnu.org/), [Clang](https://clang.llvm.org/), and [MSVC](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/cpp/). Most of the C++ in the world runs through one of them.

This makes the three implementers influential. If they won't build a feature, the standard can't force them. So a feature isn't real until it ships in a compiler you can download.

<div id="short-version">

Changing C++ is a responsibility, not a hobby. Whatever you add stays, and sixteen million people inherit it. The standard is a text, not a program, and it comes as a working draft, a published standard, and lighter tryouts. Cite its bracketed labels, not section numbers. A feature isn't real until a compiler ships it.

</div>

If all this feels heavy, good. Keep that responsibility in mind as you work your way in.

### 2.1 You Don't Need Permission

No one grants you permission to help. You won't find a gate or a gatekeeper. If you want to contribute, you start.

The real barriers are a lot smaller than the rumors make them sound. You can attend a meeting as a guest for free. You can review a proposal with nothing but a free login.

You also don't have to be a wizard. Plenty of useful work isn't writing code at all. Taking notes and reviewing other people's proposals both count.

### 2.2 The Nesting: ISO, JTC1, SC22, WG21

The committee has a long official name, and it tells a story. It's ISO/IEC JTC1/SC22/WG21. Read it left to right, and it nests from the biggest body down to the smallest.

[ISO](https://www.iso.org) is the International Organization for Standardization, which publishes standards for almost everything. With its sister body, the International Electrotechnical Commission ([IEC](https://www.iec.ch)), it runs [JTC1](https://jtc1info.org), the joint committee for information technology. That's why the name says ISO/IEC.

JTC1 holds [SC22](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/), the subcommittee for programming languages. [WG21](https://isocpp.org/std/the-committee), Working Group 21, is the part of SC22 that owns C++.

The surprising part: WG21 doesn't publish the standard (the official document that defines C++) by itself. It agrees on what C++ should be, and then the bodies above it run the official vote.

### 2.3 How the Committee Meets

The committee works in two modes. Most of the time it splits into *subgroups*, the smaller teams that each handle one slice of the work. Once in a while the whole committee gathers as one.

That full gathering is the *plenary*, where the whole committee makes its final decisions. The subgroups do the building. The plenary signs off.

This happens at a real scale. The [June 2026 meeting in Brno](https://herbsutter.com/2026/06/13/brno-trip-report/) drew about two hundred people from twenty-eight nations, a little over half in the room and the rest online. Twenty-five of them were first-time guests, so newcomers are normal here. And the nations matter as much as the people, because the official members are countries.

### 2.4 What a National Body Is

Each country takes part through its *national body*, or NB, which is the country's standards organization. The national body holds the membership and appoints the experts who attend.

Inside each national body sits a small *mirror committee* that shadows WG21 at home. That's the group you actually join. It decides who represents your country and what your country thinks of a draft.

Within WG21, people vote, not countries. In the plenary, every expert appointed by a national body votes as an individual.

Countries get their own vote later, when the finished draft goes out for a formal ballot. There, and only there, it's one country, one vote, and a delegation of twenty counts the same as a delegation of one.

You take part by joining through a national body or by visiting as a guest. Joining makes you a full member, with a path to a vote. Visiting as a guest is the quickest way to start.

### 2.5 The Easiest Start: Attending as a Guest

The lowest-cost way in is to attend as a *guest*. A guest takes part in a meeting without joining a national body. It's free.

To come as a guest, [email the convener](https://isocpp.org/std/meetings-and-participation), the person who runs WG21. Tell them you'd like to attend, and name the country where you live or work. ISO strongly encourages at least a week's notice.

As a guest you can do almost everything. You join the subgroups, argue, and take part in their polls. The one thing you can't do is vote in the plenary, where final decisions are made.

Guest status covers your first meeting. To keep coming after that, or to get the plenary vote, you need a seat through a national body. Two ways to get one are open to almost anyone.

### 2.6 Getting Further In: INCITS and the Beman Project

You don't have to be American to join through the United States. Its national body, [INCITS](https://www.incits.org/participation/apply-for-membership), is open to anyone, with or without a company behind you. Many committee members take part through it.

That matters most if your country has no mirror committee. Starting one takes about a year, so get a seat first.

INCITS charges a yearly fee per committee. In the 2026 cycle that's $2,703 at the full rate, $1,530 for small companies, and $510 for academics.

If that fee is a barrier, a free path exists. The [Beman Project](https://github.com/bemanproject) offers C++ developers free seats under its INCITS membership. You'd sit as an *alternate*, a backup member who doesn't cast Beman's vote. Travel is still on you, but the seat gets you into the rooms and the discussions.

Before you join, check who holds the seat. In some countries it's you. In the United States it's whoever pays the fee, which may be your employer. If your employer pays, the seat may not follow you to your next job.

Pick by what you need. To start cheap and fast, come as a guest. To keep going, join INCITS, or take a free Beman seat if the fee is too steep.

### 2.7 Getting a Vote: The ISO Global Directory

A vote at the plenary counts only when you're accredited. *Accredited* means your name sits in the ISO *global directory*, the official roster of voting experts. Your national body puts it there.

<div id="watch-out">

You can go to meeting after meeting and never get your name into the global directory. Then the one vote that matters to you comes up, and you have no vote to cast.

</div>

To get your name added, email the [national body chairs](https://isocpp.org/std/meetings-and-participation) and say where you live or work. Some national bodies run one committee for every programming language, so say you want C++. A guest needs a week's notice. A vote needs months, so write long before the meeting where you want one.

A seat asks two things of you: read the proposals before you vote, and stay active in your mirror committee. You vote as yourself, but you're there on your national body's word.

A vote is only one way to matter. Many roles need no vote at all.

### 2.8 The Roles You Can Play

You can help in more ways than you'd guess. The easiest roles need no membership at all. Here are the main ones:

- Note-taker, also called the scribe. Every session needs minutes, and anyone can be asked to take them. It's a clear way to be useful on day one.
- Reviewer. Read a proposal that's coming up and post what you think. A free login is all you need, and giving reviews earns you reviews of your own.
- Author. Write up an idea and submit it for the committee to consider.
- Implementer. Build part of a feature in a compiler and report what you learn.
- Champion. Present a proposal and push it through the rooms. A *champion* can be the author, or someone else who believes in the idea.

Notice that the first two need nothing but your time and attention.

### 2.9 Your First Day: Orientation and Introductions

Your first meeting starts before the work does. At 6 p.m. on the Sunday before, there's a newcomer orientation, which usually meets in the main hotel's lobby. It covers how the week works and answers your questions.

Soon after, you'll get a welcome along with the other first-timers. You may be asked to say who you are and where you're from. It's brief and friendly, not a test.

After the welcome, one habit matters more than any other.

### 2.10 Showing Up Is the Secret

The single biggest thing you can do is keep showing up. An old line says "eighty percent of success is showing up." In this committee, it's close to true.

Showing up builds two things: familiarity and trust. People learn your name and your judgment over many meetings. The committee runs on relationships, and relationships need time.

The reverse is true too. When a regular voice stops attending, the committee quietly loses what that person brought. Presence matters here more than brilliance.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

Show up, take notes, and read the proposals. Do those three things for three meetings, and you'll be a known quantity.

</div>

### 2.11 Where Ideas Start

A lot of C++ ideas start with an everyday problem. A product hits a limit in the language, a bug keeps coming back, or people keep writing the same code by hand. Sometimes a programmer just needs sockets, finds the standard library has none, and asks why not.

So if you want an idea worth chasing, look at what your code and your users can't do, not at what would be clever.

Conferences are where those ideas get aired. Many committee members go, so a conference is an easy place to meet them in a relaxed setting.

Two stand out. [C++Now](https://cppnow.org) is small and expert-heavy, held each spring in Aspen, Colorado. It's where people push and refine new ideas. [CppCon](https://cppcon.org) is the big one, open to everyone, where ideas meet the wider community.

You don't have to fly to the United States, either. [isocpp.org keeps a worldwide list](https://isocpp.org/wiki/faq/conferences-worldwide), with [ACCU](https://accu.org) and [Meeting C++](https://meetingcpp.com) among the European ones. Going to any of them is a low-pressure way to start.

<div id="short-version">

You don't need anyone's permission to start. Within WG21, which sits inside ISO/IEC JTC1/SC22, people vote, not countries. Countries vote later, on the formal ballot. You can come to your first meeting free, as a guest, with a week's notice. Guests do everything but vote at the plenary.

To keep coming, you need a seat through a national body. INCITS takes anyone for a fee, and the Beman Project offers free seats if that's too steep. A plenary vote also needs your name in the global directory, and that takes months, so ask early.

Plenty of the work needs no vote at all. Take notes, review proposals, and keep showing up.

</div>

Nobody's guarding the entrance. Next up: the thing all this work revolves around - the paper.

### 3.1 Nothing Happens Without a Paper

One rule explains most of the committee: no paper, no proposal. The rulebook, [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures), puts it bluntly: "if a proposal doesn't have a paper, it doesn't exist." A *paper* is a written proposal or report, and it's the unit of all the work.

A paper needs three things before a room can act on it. The paper has to exist, it has to arrive on time, and someone has to be there to present it. Miss one and the room moves on.

Talk still matters. A hallway conversation can expose a flaw, improve a design, or change a mind. But the room only votes on what it can read, so if you want to move the committee, write it down.

### 3.2 What a Paper Is and Its Types

Papers sort first by what they want from you. An *ask-paper* proposes something and asks for a poll. An *inform-paper* puts facts on the record and asks for nothing. Those aren't official labels, just useful ones.

Here's a quiet truth: the inform-paper often gets read more. It asks for no time on a crowded schedule. So it slips in where an ask-paper waits in line.

Papers also split by job. Some argue for a design, and others nail down exact standard wording. A separate kind, the *standing document*, holds the committee's own rules instead of proposing anything.

Whatever its kind, every paper goes by a number, and the number itself tells you a lot.

### 3.3 The P-Number System

A paper's number starts with a letter that tells you what sort of document it is. A *P-number* marks a proposal, like [P2300](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2024/p2300r10.html). A *D-number* marks a draft that hasn't been published yet.

An *N-number* marks an official ISO document, like the working draft itself, an agenda, or minutes. Before 2015 every paper got one, so an older N-number is often a proposal.

After the number comes a *revision*, like R0 for the first version, then R1, R2, and up. Read the newest one you can find.

R0 is brand new and untested in the room. A paper at R5 has been reworked five times.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

A paper's revision number tells you its history, not its quality. A fifth revision means someone kept working, not that they got it right. Judge what's on the page.

</div>

Once you have a number, the paper is one link away.

### 3.4 Finding Any Paper

You don't hunt for papers by hand. Three sites will find you any paper, but only one is the real archive. That's [open-std.org](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/), where every paper lives in a plain list by date. Every paper link in this notebook points there.

A friendlier view of the same papers is [wg21.org](https://wg21.org). It's searchable and sorted by group, and each entry links back to the original on open-std.org. Treat it as a window onto the archive, not the archive itself.

The quickest route is wg21.link, an unofficial shortcut that Mara Bos has run for years. Type a paper number after the slash, in lowercase, and it sends you to the copy on open-std.org. For P2300 revision 10, that's `wg21.link/p2300r10`.

### 3.5 The Mailing System and Deadlines

Papers arrive in batches called *the mailing*, most months of the year. The biggest ones land just before and just after each meeting. In 2025 there were nine in all. Counting revisions, that's eight hundred or more papers a year.

Timing decides what the committee can act on. Each meeting has a *pre-meeting mailing deadline*, weeks ahead of it. Miss it and your paper sits out until the next round.

From that first mailing, a paper begins a long journey.

### 3.6 The Paper Lifecycle

A paper that makes it follows a set route called *the paper lifecycle*. It starts as an idea and ends in the published standard. Most papers stop somewhere along the way.

The ones that finish pass through the same stages, in the same order:

1. You submit the paper to a mailing.
2. A small group incubates the idea and shapes it.
3. A design group decides whether the direction is right.
4. A wording group turns the approved design into exact standard text.
5. The plenary, the full committee, votes it into the working draft (the living text that becomes the next standard).
6. The national bodies review the whole draft and send comments, and the committee answers every one. [C++26 drew 411 of them](https://herbsutter.com/2026/03/29/c26-is-done-trip-report-march-2026-iso-c-standards-meeting-london-croydon-uk/).
7. The national bodies vote to approve it, and ISO publishes the new standard.

Any stage can be the end. A paper can stall, get sent back, or quietly die partway through. The whole journey takes years.

The committee keeps a longer, [stage-by-stage version](https://isocpp.org/std/the-life-of-an-iso-proposal) of this path, and it's worth reading once.

### 3.7 Design Review versus Wording Review

Two of those stages, design and wording, ask very different questions. *Design review* asks: is this the right feature, built the right way? *Wording review* asks: is the exact text precise and complete?

These take different mindsets. Design review argues over whether C++ should have the feature and what shape it should take. Wording review assumes the answer is yes and hunts for gaps in the spec text.

The two happen in order. First a design group approves the direction. Then a wording group takes over and writes the standard text.

Not every paper proposes something new. Some just fix mistakes.

### 3.8 Defect Reports and the Issues Lists

The standard ships with bugs like anything else. Anyone can report one as an *issue*, and the committee decides whether it needs a fix. Once the full committee accepts a fix, the issue becomes a *defect report*, a wording repair rather than a new feature.

These bugs are tracked in public on two *issues lists*, one for the [core language](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/cwg_active.html) and one for the [library](https://cplusplus.github.io/LWG/lwg-active.html). The wording groups work through them, and the fixes the plenary approves go into the working draft.

### 3.9 Reading a Paper Critically

You can't read eight hundred papers a year, so don't try. Read the ones in your subgroup (the smaller group you follow) and skim the rest. Read deeply only where you'll vote.

Always read the newest revision. An old one makes you raise a concern the author already fixed. When a paper comes back, compare it against the last version to see what changed.

Before you form an opinion on any paper, run it through a few questions:

- What problem does it solve, and does it say so?
- Does it weigh other options, or pretend none exist?
- Has anyone built it, or is it only an idea?
- What old code breaks, and how do users move forward?
- Is the wording clear on the hard cases?

The gaps are the red flags. Empty answers, like no implementation and no migration plan, are objections waiting on the floor. A strong paper closes them before you ask. If one doesn't, tell the author.

<div id="try-this-today">

Open the newest mailing on open-std.org and pick one paper on something you use every day. Ask what it fixes, what it breaks, and whether anyone has built it. Write down one gap. That's how every review starts.

</div>

Two documents will save you more than any single paper.

### 3.10 The Documents Everyone Should Read: SD-4 and P2000

Before almost anything else, read the rulebook and the roadmap. The rulebook is SD-4, "WG21 Practices and Procedures," the practices everyone is expected to know. It covers polls, consensus, deadlines, ballots, and how to escalate a disagreement.

The roadmap is [P2000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf), "Direction for ISO C++." A small group of experienced members called the *Direction Group* writes it to recommend where the committee should head over the long run. Reading it tells you which way the wind is blowing.

<div id="short-version">

No paper, no proposal. Every paper lives on open-std.org, and its number tells you its kind and history. New papers arrive in mailings, and a late one sits out a meeting. Most papers never finish the years-long journey to the standard. Read the newest revision critically. And before almost anything else, read SD-4 and P2000.

</div>

All the committee's work flows through papers, and now you know how to read one. Sooner or later you'll want to say something about what you've read, and that's where taking part begins.

## 4. How to Participate Remotely

You don't have to fly anywhere to start. Most of the committee's life happens online, between meetings. All you need is a desk and a browser.

<div id="in-this-chapter">

- What you can do with no plane ticket at all
- The reflector: what it is, who gets in, what you can't quote, and why courtesy is the rule
- The three websites, and which ones are official
- The wiki and the chat, and when they open up to you
- Where to float an idea before it becomes a paper
- Telecons, electronic polls, and following a live meeting
- Open-source ways to help

</div>

### 4.1 You Can Take Part from Your Desk

Plenty of useful work happens with no plane ticket. You can read, review, and discuss from home, and you can even take the minutes for an online meeting. None of it needs a meeting badge.

The lowest-effort start is reviewing a proposal. You read one that's coming up and post what you think. A free login is all it takes, and giving reviews earns you reviews of your own.

### 4.2 The Reflector

The committee's main online home is email. Its mailing lists are called *the reflector*, and most discussion between meetings happens there. Each subgroup (smaller group) keeps its own list.

You can join the lists once you've attended any meeting, in person or online. Before that, many topic lists are [readable in public](https://lists.isocpp.org/mailman/listinfo.cgi), and the person who runs a topic group can add an expert directly.

One rule matters from day one. Reflector posts are private, so you can't quote them in public. You can share poll questions and their numbers freely once the meeting is over, but a person's words need that person's consent.

Be courteous on the lists, especially when you're sure you're right. Email strips the tone out of an argument and leaves only the words.

The people reading yours will run your sessions and review your papers for years.

<div id="watch-out">

A sharp reply on the lists feels good for a minute and costs you a reader for a decade.

</div>

### 4.3 The Official Sites, and One Unofficial One

Three websites cover most of what you need. [isocpp.org](https://isocpp.org) is the public-facing site, home to the standing documents and committee news. Start there for anything official.

[open-std.org](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/) is the official archive of every paper and draft. It's plain and sorted by date, with no search. It's the source of truth, even if it's bare.

[wg21.org](https://wg21.org) is the unofficial one, a friendlier way into the same papers. Volunteers built it to search and sort the mailing by group. The papers still live on open-std.org, and this view only makes them easier to find.

### 4.4 The Wiki

The committee runs its meetings off *the wiki*. It holds the agendas, schedules, video-call links, and poll pages for each meeting. If you attend, you'll live in it that week.

Two things to know. Don't edit the wiki unless someone asks you to. And you'll get access once you're a participant, not before.

### 4.5 Real-Time Chat: Mattermost and Discord

When things move fast, people use chat. [Mattermost](https://chat.isocpp.org), at chat.isocpp.org, is the committee's own real-time chat. During meetings it keeps the parallel rooms in touch. It's for committee participants, so it opens up once you're taking part.

For the wider community, the [#include \<C++\> Discord](https://www.includecpp.org) is open to anyone. It's friendly, public, and full of people who'll answer questions. It's a gentle place to learn the culture.

The courtesy rule from the reflector holds in every chat, too. Chat is faster than email and even easier to misread.

### 4.6 Floating Ideas: std-proposals

Before you write a formal proposal, float the idea first. [std-proposals](https://lists.isocpp.org/mailman/listinfo.cgi/std-proposals) is a public list for early-stage ideas. You describe your idea and see what people think.

This step saves you months. You learn fast whether anyone wants the idea, and you get early feedback that makes the real proposal stronger.

<div id="try-this-today">

Subscribe to std-proposals today and read it for a week before you post. You'll see what a warm reception looks like, and a cold one, before your own idea gets either.

</div>

### 4.7 Telecons Between Meetings

The big meetings happen three times a year, but the work never stops. Between them, subgroups hold online meetings called *telecons*, [about thirty a month](https://isocpp.org/std/meetings-and-participation) across the various groups.

You find telecons on the committee's shared calendar. The wiki lists the schedule and the links. Pick a subgroup you care about and sit in.

### 4.8 Voting from Afar: Electronic Polls

Not every vote waits for a meeting. Some groups, the library side especially, run polls online between meetings. They use these to settle smaller questions without burning floor time.

The rules are in [P2195R2](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2021/p2195r2.html), "Electronic Straw Polls." Each round and its results are published as papers, so you can follow along from anywhere. The May 2025 round, for example, is [P3712R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2025/p3712r0.html), and its outcomes are in [P3713R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2025/p3713r0.html).

### 4.9 Following a Live Meeting

Meetings are hybrid, so you can join the rooms by video. You won't catch everything from afar. The hallway talk, where a lot gets settled, is hard to join remotely.

After each meeting, the results come out fast. Several delegates post public trip reports, like [Herb Sutter's blog](https://herbsutter.com). The official minutes come out as an N-paper, like [N5040](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/n5040.pdf) for the March 2026 meeting, so the record is there to read.

One catch if you're far away is time zones. A meeting on another continent can run through your night. Pick the sessions worth losing sleep over, and read the rest later.

### 4.10 Open-Source Help: The Beman Project and Tools

You can help with code without ever attending. The [Beman Project](https://github.com/bemanproject) builds open-source implementations of proposed standard library features, so people can try them for real. You can help write and test them, or use them in your own code and report what breaks. Contributing there is contributing to the standard's future.

Small tools make committee life easier, too. The handiest is wg21.link, from the last chapter, which turns any paper number into a link. You'll pick up more as you go.

<div id="short-version">

You can read, review, and discuss from home. Once you've attended any meeting, in person or online, you can join the reflector. Its posts are private. Poll questions and their numbers are fine to share once the meeting is over. To quote someone's words, ask them first. Stay courteous on the lists and in chat.

The official sites are isocpp.org and open-std.org, and wg21.org is an unofficial, friendlier index. The wiki and Mattermost open once you're a participant. Float ideas on std-proposals, sit in on telecons (about thirty a month), join live meetings by video, and read the trip reports. Or help write and test the Beman Project's libraries.

</div>

Distance is no excuse. You can start today, from your desk. Next, let's map the rooms where all this work gets sorted.

## 5. How WG21 Is Structured

WG21 looks like a wall of acronyms at first. EWG, LEWG, and fifteen study groups blur together. This chapter turns that wall into a clear map.

<div id="in-this-chapter">

- The rooms: two that design, two that word, and the study groups that feed them
- How a paper climbs from one room to the next
- The train model, and where it came from
- Why features almost never come out once they're in
- Who runs the committee, and who appoints them
- Who sets the committee's priorities
- How much power a chair really has, and the rule they schedule by
- The standing documents, where the committee keeps its rules
- Why nobody can be ordered to do anything, and the implementer veto

</div>

### 5.1 One Committee, Many Rooms

WG21 does its work in several groups people call *the rooms*. Each room owns one slice of the job. They meet at the same time, in parallel, all week.

That parallel timetable is the *schedule grid*. Six or seven rooms run at once, so you can't attend them all. You pick one room and follow it.

The rooms split into two jobs. Some decide what to build (design), and others write the exact words (wording).

### 5.2 The Evolution Rooms: EWG and LEWG

Two rooms decide whether a feature is worth building. *EWG*, the Evolution Working Group, handles the core language. It judges proposed language features.

*LEWG*, the Library Evolution Working Group, does the same for the standard library. It decides which library features the committee wants. Both rooms answer the design question, not the wording one.

### 5.3 The Wording Rooms: CWG and LWG

Two more rooms turn approved designs into exact standard text. *CWG*, the Core Working Group, reviews and polishes the language wording. It hunts for gaps and ambiguity in the spec.

*LWG*, the Library Working Group, does the same for the library. It's careful, detailed work on the precise words. These rooms ask whether the text says what the design rooms approved, not whether the feature is wanted.

### 5.4 Study Groups

New ideas usually start in a *study group*, or SG. A study group focuses on one area, like concurrency or networking, and shapes raw ideas.

They're numbered SG1 through SG23. Fifteen are active as of 2026, and the rest sit dormant until new papers wake them up. The [committee page](https://isocpp.org/std/the-committee) lists them all.

Each study group is a world of its own, deep in its specialty. When an idea is ready, it graduates to a design room. The convener (the person who runs WG21) creates and closes study groups as needs change.

### 5.5 How a Paper Moves Between Groups

A paper passes through a chain of groups. A study group shapes it, a design room approves it, a wording room finalizes it, and the plenary (the full committee) adopts it. Each handoff is a vote to send it onward.

Some papers touch more than one room. A feature that changes the language and the library visits both evolution rooms, sometimes together in a joint session. The groups coordinate so nothing falls through the cracks.

### 5.6 The Train Model

C++ ships on a fixed schedule, and that rule is *the train model*. Every three years a new standard leaves the station with whatever features are ready. C++14, 17, 20, 23, and 26 all rode it.

Herb Sutter proposed it in 2011 in [N3316](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2011/n3316.pdf). Before it, C++11 slipped for years. It's been the model since 2012, and [P1000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p1000r7.pdf) lays out the current timetable. The schedule traded "now or never" for "catch the next train."

The model has a safety valve: pull what isn't ready. The committee did exactly that in 2019 with [P1823](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2019/p1823r0.pdf), taking contracts out of C++20 to let them bake longer. The valve works, but pulling a big feature is never cheap.

### 5.7 How Features Get Removed

Taking a feature out is far harder than putting one in. Once a feature ships, real programs depend on it, and breaking them isn't an option. So the standard almost only grows.

There's a difference between pulling and removing. Pulling takes a feature out before it ships, which happens now and then. Removing something already shipped is rare, and it can take many years.

### 5.8 The People Who Run It

A handful of officers keep the committee running. The convener appoints the chairs, creates study groups, sets the schedule, and judges whether the full committee agrees. [SC22](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/), WG21's parent committee, appoints the convener for terms of up to three years, with no limit on renewals. Two vice-conveners and a secretary help run things.

Two *project editors* keep the working draft. They're the people who apply every approved change to the text of the standard. It's quiet, exacting work that holds the whole document together.

Each room has its own small crew. A *chair* runs the session, and someone takes the minutes. Anyone in the room can be asked to scribe, so it might be you.

### 5.9 Who Sets Priorities: The Direction Group

A small senior group, the Direction Group from chapter 3, steers the committee's focus. It writes down where C++ should go in [P2000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf) and what the next standard should put first in [P5000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p5000r1.pdf). Membership is by invitation, not election.

The Direction Group doesn't decide individual papers. It sets the big-picture priorities that chairs lean on when scheduling. Framing your work to match those priorities helps it get attention.

### 5.10 Chair Power

The chair of a room holds more power than it looks. The chair decides which papers make the agenda, when they come up, how the polls are worded, and when the room has agreed. None of that is a vote, yet all of it shapes outcomes.

The strongest power is the calendar. A chair who never schedules a paper can stall it for years, without ever voting against it. So getting on the agenda is its own battle.

The calendar does have rules, though. [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) tells chairs what comes first: work tied to the Direction Group's priorities, work aimed at the next standard, and work that can make progress at this meeting. Everything else gets time as time allows.

You can watch the queue yourself. Every paper gets a tracking issue in a public list on [GitHub](https://github.com/cplusplus/papers). It shows you what's waiting and what's moving.

### 5.11 The Rules: Standing Documents

The committee's own rules live in [standing documents](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents) (the numbered SD files from chapter 3). Eight are current, and the numbering has gaps because older ones were retired. They run from study-group setup to how to write papers. The main one is SD-4, the practices and procedures.

Standing documents change slowly and carefully. New ones need the full committee's agreement. A standing document only goes away when someone retires it, so old practices tend to stay.

### 5.12 The Limits of Power: Volunteers and the Implementer Veto

Here's the truth under all the structure: nobody can be ordered to do anything. WG21 is volunteers, and it only makes recommendations.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

A proposal lives only as long as someone keeps showing up for it. Nobody can be assigned to do that for you. If it's your idea, the someone is you.

</div>

The compiler teams hold a quiet *implementer veto*. If GCC, Clang, and MSVC (the three implementers from chapter 1) won't build a feature, the standard can't make them.

It's a veto over what you can use, not over what gets adopted. The committee can standardize a feature nobody has built yet. Then the standard says one thing, your compiler does another, and you live in the gap.

This isn't only theory. In January 2026, eighteen implementers wrote [P3962](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p3962r0.pdf) asking the committee to slow down, because features pile up faster than they can build them. The people who turn the standard real are stretched thin.

<div id="short-version">

EWG and LEWG decide what to build, CWG and LWG write the exact words, study groups feed them, and the plenary adopts. The train model ships C++ every three years with whatever's ready, and shipped features almost never come back out.

The convener runs the committee, the Direction Group sets priorities, and the chairs control the calendar. The rules live in eight standing documents, SD-4 above all. Nobody can be ordered to do anything. The compiler teams can refuse to build a feature. That's a veto over what you can use, not over what gets adopted.

</div>

Now the acronyms have a shape. You can see where a paper goes and who decides. Next, let's get you to an actual meeting.

## 6. Getting There

Going to your first meeting is a leap, and this chapter is your running start. None of it is as scary as it feels from the outside.

<div id="in-this-chapter">

- What a six-day meeting week looks like
- The three things to do before you fly
- What the trip costs in money and time, and who usually pays
- What to pack, including patience
- Why your first meeting will overwhelm you, and why that's fine

</div>

### 6.1 What a Meeting Week Looks Like

The committee meets in person three times a year, and the [upcoming meetings page](https://isocpp.org/std/meetings-and-participation/upcoming-meetings) lists the dates and places. Each one is a *meeting week*, running Monday through Saturday. Days are long, roughly 8 a.m. to 5 p.m., with optional evening sessions on top. Saturday wraps up by 2 p.m.

During the week, the rooms run in parallel all day, six or seven at once. A short plenary opens the week on Monday morning, and everything builds toward the closing plenary on Saturday, where adopted wording enters the working draft. It's a marathon, not a sprint.

A good week starts before you arrive.

### 6.2 Preparing Before You Go

The best preparation is reading. Pick one subgroup (smaller group) to focus on, and read its agenda papers before you arrive. You can't follow six rooms, so don't try.

<div id="try-this-today">

Pick the one room you'd follow if you flew tomorrow. Open the latest mailing, find three papers meant for it, and start the first one tonight. Arriving with opinions about three papers beats arriving with none about thirty.

</div>

Two more steps round it out. Read [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) so the polls and rules don't surprise you. Check the wiki for the schedule, the rooms, and the video links.

Preparation is free. The trip itself is not.

### 6.3 The Cost and How to Pay for It

Attending has no registration fee. What costs money is the trip: flights, a week of hotel nights, and meals, which together can run into the thousands. Each meeting has a logistics paper, like [N5057](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/n5057.pdf) for spring 2027, that tells you what to expect before you book. [SD-5](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-5-meeting-information), "Meeting Information," covers the rest.

The bigger cost is often the week itself, away from your job. There's one more that nobody puts on the bill: three weeks a year far from home. Even the evenings belong to the meeting. Count that before you count the airfare.

People cover the expenses in a few ways. Many are sponsored by an employer who values the work, and others pay their own way.

If the national body fee is what's stopping you, the free guest and Beman paths from chapter 2 remove it. The trip is still yours to cover.

Once the trip is booked, packing is quick.

### 6.4 What to Bring

Pack light, but pack a laptop. You'll read papers, follow along, and maybe take notes, all on screen. Bring its charger and a plug adapter for the host country.

Bring patience too. The pace is slow and the debates run long. That's the work, not a sign something's wrong.

### 6.5 Bracing for Your First Meeting

Your first meeting will overwhelm you. Six rooms run at once, hundreds of papers are in play, and everyone seems to know each other. That's normal, and it passes.

<div id="watch-out">

Don't grade your first meeting by what you got done. Go in to win something, and six rooms will scatter you. You'll fly home feeling you failed. Your first meeting is for watching and learning. The payoff is your second one, when you walk in knowing the room.

</div>

Set your goal low on purpose. Pick one room, follow it, and let the rest wash over you.

You're not the odd one out. About two dozen new guests show up at every meeting, just as you will. The Sunday orientation exists because everyone starts here.

<div id="short-version">

A meeting week runs from a short plenary Monday to the closing one Saturday, with six or seven rooms running in between. Prepare by reading one room's papers and SD-4. The week away often costs more than the trip. Bring a laptop and patience. Expect to be overwhelmed, and aim to watch, not to win.

</div>

You're as ready as anyone is for a first meeting. Forgive yourself now for everything you'll miss. Next, let's learn how to act once you're in the room.

## 7. In the Room

The first time in the room, you'll worry about doing something wrong. Don't. Once you know the unwritten rules, and a few written ones, you can relax and pay attention to the work.

<div id="in-this-chapter">

- What the opening plenary is for, and why it's short
- How to move between rooms without walking in cold
- The small courtesies that keep a room running
- How to speak so the scribe and remote attendees can follow
- What stays in the room, and how everyone is expected to behave
- Why taking minutes teaches you the room
- Where relationships form, in the hallway and over meals
- Why you should get out of your lane and stay skeptical

</div>

### 7.1 The Opening Plenary

The week opens with the whole committee in one room. This is the *opening plenary*. The officers say hello, new members introduce themselves, the agenda and the updated working drafts get approved, and then everyone scatters to the subgroups.

Don't expect drama here. The opening plenary is short and mostly logistics. The real work happens in the rooms right after.

### 7.2 Cycling Between Rooms

You can be in only one room at a time, and six or seven run at once. So follow the schedule, not a single room. Go where the paper you care about is being discussed.

Whatever room you pick, arrive having read its papers. Walking in cold is the classic rookie mistake. The discussion moves fast and won't recap for you.

### 7.3 Meeting Etiquette

The room runs on small courtesies. Don't dominate the discussion, and don't reopen a point the room has moved past. Keep your laptop for notes, not for distractions that pull your focus.

Listen more than you talk at first. Spend your early sessions learning how the room thinks.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

You'll earn your voice faster by using it sparingly. Save it for the point nobody else in the room is making.

</div>

### 7.4 How to Speak

When you do speak, a little technique goes a long way. In small rooms you usually raise a hand, and in big ones you line up at a microphone. Make one point, and keep it to about two minutes. The room has a lot to get through, and short is respected.

Two habits help everyone follow you. Say your name before you start, because the scribe and the remote attendees need it. Use the microphone, because without it the online half of the room can't hear you.

### 7.5 Confidentiality and the Code of Conduct

What happens in the room mostly stays in the room. The *confidentiality rule* means you don't blog, tweet, photograph, or record the sessions, because people need to speak freely. Treat everything said as off the public record by default.

Two narrow exceptions exist. You can share poll questions and their numbers freely once the meeting is over. The March 2026 minutes, [N5040](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/n5040.pdf), spell that out. You can quote a person by name only with that person's consent, which is rarely asked and rarely given, so paraphrase instead.

WG21 follows two codes together: the [ISO Code of Ethics and Conduct](https://www.iso.org/publication/PUB100011.html) and the [IEC Code of Conduct](https://iec.ch/basecamp/iec-code-conduct-technical-work). They cover how people treat each other everywhere the committee gathers, including chat, meals, and social events, and they apply on social media too. The short version is to be decent and assume good faith.

A slide at the start of every meeting sums them up. In a room drawn from dozens of countries, one line matters most: "be aware that jokes and humor may not translate." If something goes wrong, [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) points you to ISO's process for reporting a concern.

### 7.6 Note-Taking

Every session has to be minuted, and anyone can be asked to do it. SD-4 says everyone present "should be prepared to take minutes." So you might be handed the keyboard on day one. Say yes.

The minute taker is called the scribe, and it's the best seat in the house for you. Writing an argument down is different from hearing it, because you have to understand it well enough to sum it up. A week of it teaches you what your room is wrestling with. People also remember who helped.

The scribe also gets a small superpower. Everyone else waits their turn to speak, but you can skip the queue. If you missed a point, ask the speaker to say it again, because the minutes have to be right. That lets you ask the question everyone else was too proud to ask.

### 7.7 Where Relationships Form

The hallway is not break time. A lot of the real work happens there, between sessions. People sort out disagreements over coffee that would stall a formal room.

Meals work the same way. Lunch and dinner groups form on their own, so join one. Introducing yourself over a meal is normal and expected, not pushy.

<div id="watch-out">

By midweek, the people at your lunch table will feel like friends. That's the good part, and it's also the trap. Liking people makes it easy to agree with them. Start nodding along, and you lose the one thing you walked in with: a fresh eye.

</div>

### 7.8 Get Out of Your Lane

Spend some time in rooms outside your specialty. The Direction Group's [P2000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf) puts it plainly: "try to spend at least one day each meeting in a WG that isn't 'your own.'" You'll build trust across groups and see how the whole machine fits together.

Two habits earn respect fast. Serve before you push: help onboard newcomers and explain procedure. And when someone says "that's just how it works," ask how other groups like the [IETF](https://www.ietf.org) or [W3C](https://www.w3.org) handle the same thing.

Above all, keep your wits about you. The meeting has a rhythm that can sweep you up. Stay skeptical, and question the room's assumptions, including your own.

<div id="short-version">

Follow the papers you care about from room to room, and read them before you walk in. Listen more than you talk at first. When you do speak, say your name, use the microphone, and make one short point.

Keep what's said in the room off the public record, and be decent to everyone. Say yes when you're asked to take minutes. Get to know people over meals, and spend a day outside your own room. Through it all, question the room's assumptions and your own.

</div>

Now you know the room's rhythm and its rules. You can stop worrying about doing something wrong and pay attention to the work. The next mystery is how all this talk turns into decisions - the committee's voting process.

## 8. How Decisions Get Made

Voting here doesn't work the way you'd expect. No majority rules, and no simple count wins. This chapter shows you how the committee really decides, so you can read a room and vote with a clear head.

- How a small minority can block, and how to escalate properly
- What actually happens at the closing plenary
- How old bugs in the standard get fixed
- The two national ballots, and which one changes the text

### 8.1 The Culture of Consensus

The committee decides by *consensus*, which isn't what most people think. It doesn't mean everyone agrees, and it doesn't mean the majority wins. It means no strong, sustained opposition is left standing. That's ISO's definition, passed down through [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures), the practices guide from chapter 3.

The test is whether people can live with a decision. A few mild objections don't block it. A small group of firm, reasoned objections can.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

Consensus is social pressure with a polite name. Keep your wits about you, and don't let the room's mood stand in for your own judgment.

</div>

The committee measures consensus with one simple tool: the straw poll.

### 8.2 Straw Polls and the Five-Point Scale

To read the room, the chair takes a *straw poll*. In the usual kind, you pick one of five positions: strongly favor, weakly favor, neutral, weakly against, or strongly against. Where the room clearly agrees, the chair may just ask whether anyone objects.

In a subgroup, everyone in the room can vote, guests included. The chair weighs the discussion and the numbers, then decides whether there's consensus. No fixed number settles it, so the chair judges.

### 8.3 Reading Poll Results

A rough rule guides the call. A proposal normally advances when those in favor outnumber those against by more than two to one. This is the *2:1 guideline*, and "normally" is doing a lot of work.

The count comes after the room hears out the against votes, maybe with a second poll. Neutrals stay out of the ratio, but a mostly neutral room is a warning sign.

But the count isn't everything. Because the bar is no sustained opposition, a block of strong-against votes can sink a proposal even when far more people favor it.

This really happens. In a January 2022 online poll, one large library proposal for C++23 drew 37 in favor and 17 against. It [still failed](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2022/p2459r0.html) for "sustained strong opposition." More than two to one wasn't enough.

Straw polls steer the work, but they're not the binding vote.

### 8.4 Straw Poll versus Formal Vote

A straw poll is a temperature check, not a decision. The binding kind is a *formal vote*, taken at plenary and on the national ballots. One steers the work, the other commits it.

Still, take straw polls seriously. The committee treats them as honest predictors of the formal vote to come. How you vote in a subgroup signals where you'll land later.

Whichever vote it is, you face the same question: should you vote at all?

### 8.5 When to Vote and When to Abstain

Vote your honest position when you have one. If you favor something, say so, not neutral. Neutral means you read the paper, weighed the issues, and truly land in the middle, which should be rare.

If you vote against, be ready to say why, and to name what would change your mind.

When the topic is outside what you know, *abstain*. Abstaining means not voting at all, and it's the honest move on a paper you haven't read. Hearing a summary isn't the same as knowing the issue.

<div id="watch-out">

Voting with the room on a paper you haven't read feels safe, and raising your hand feels like belonging. But the chair reads every hand as judgment, so a borrowed vote is noise that still counts. Read it or don't vote.

</div>

The Direction Group (the senior group from chapter 5) makes the matching point about voting against. Its paper, [P2000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf), says that not having time to read an on-time paper is no reason to oppose it, and that people who sat out the discussion should "at least stay neutral." The point is not to stand in the way, and abstaining doesn't. So don't oppose what you haven't read, and don't support it either.

Whatever you vote, ask for evidence. Don't vote yes on enthusiasm or a friend's nod. Be moved by proof, not by the mood of the room.

Votes also come in two flavors that newcomers mix up.

### 8.6 Two Gates: Direction versus Design

A proposal passes through two different gates. *Direction approval* is early: does the committee want this kind of thing at all? *Design approval* is later: is this specific design the right one?

Here's the trap. Passing either gate is not a promise to ship. A warm direction poll can sit for years with nothing delivered.

Read encouragement for what it is. "We'd like to see more work" means keep going, not "this will ship." Expansion statements, [P1306R1](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2019/p1306r1.pdf), won approval for C++20 in 2019 from EWG, the language design room. They missed C++20 anyway and were adopted for C++26 in 2025, six years later.

Because the bar is no sustained opposition, a few people hold real power.

### 8.7 A Small Minority Can Block

A small group can stop a lot. Since consensus means no sustained opposition, a handful of firm objectors can block a proposal the majority wants. This is built into the system, not a loophole.

It cuts both ways, though. SD-4 says consensus is "not a tyranny of the majority," and it's "not a tyranny of the minority" either. Once objectors have been heard, they can't just keep saying no.

Find the objections early. Learn who has concerns and talk to them before the poll, not after. A lone objector is weaker than one who's found an ally on the merits.

If you still think a serious mistake is being made, there's a short *escalation path* with a hard deadline. Tell the EWG and LEWG chairs as early as you can, post the concern on the subgroup's reflector by 5 p.m. the evening before the closing plenary, and raise it with your national body. At plenary the convener may ask your delegation's chair whether the objection is personal or national, and it's only national if the delegation has already talked it through.

An objection that skips these steps gets no weight at plenary, new or repeated. Once the deadline passes, the concern has to wait for a later round, either a new reflector thread or a national ballot comment, backed by at least a draft paper.

All these subgroup votes feed one place: the plenary.

### 8.8 The Plenary: Where Features Enter the Draft

The week ends with the *closing plenary* on Saturday. Each subgroup reports, and the committee polls on motions to adopt finished wording. A feature enters the working draft the moment its motion passes.

Plenary usually runs on silence. The convener (the person who runs WG21) asks if anyone objects, and if no one does, the motion passes. Raising a hand to object takes no explanation.

An objection forces a three-way poll: favor, against, or neutral. Only registered members vote, so guests watch. The convener then judges whether there's consensus. Plenary is where objections are counted, not where they're born.

Passing the subgroups isn't the final word. A proposal can clear every room and still fail at plenary. The type `std::byte` did exactly that in November 2016, [failing with 24 in favor and 15 against](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2016/n4623.pdf), after a late fight over its name.

It came back at the next meeting and [passed 44 to 2](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2017/n4654.pdf), name unchanged.

Plenary handles new features. A quieter process handles old bugs.

### 8.9 Processing Issues

Not every decision is about new features. The wording rooms, CWG and LWG, work steadily through the [core](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/cwg_active.html) and [library](https://cplusplus.github.io/LWG/lwg-active.html) issues lists, the standard's bug trackers. They settle each defect's exact fix, then move the ready ones at plenary [in one batch](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2025/n5015.html).

New features and fixes alike still face one last hurdle: the national ballots.

### 8.10 The Countries Vote

Before the standard is final, the countries vote, and each one gets a single vote. They usually see the draft twice.

First it goes out as a *committee draft* for comments. That's the round meant for changing the text. For C++26, it drew 411 comments. Then the cleaned-up draft goes out for approval as a *DIS* (Draft International Standard). For C++26, that ballot ran from July 31 to October 9, 2026.

Passing takes a strong majority. Two-thirds of the voting countries must approve, and no more than a quarter of the votes can be *no*.

A country with concerns usually votes *yes* and attaches comments. A *no* is blunt. SD-4 says it means the country doesn't want the work "to progress at all in any form." Every comment gets considered whatever the vote, so a *no* buys nothing extra.

If the DIS passes and the text needs no technical changes, the draft goes straight to publication. Technical changes trigger one last, shorter vote on a near-final *FDIS* (Final Draft International Standard). The committee aims to make the DIS its last ballot, so a design change this late needs nearly everyone on board.

That first round is where you can help. A good comment works like a small paper: it says what's wrong, where, and what text would fix it. Drafting one for your national body takes no seniority.

<div id="short-version">

Consensus means no sustained opposition is left standing, not that the majority wins. In a straw poll, more than two to one in favor usually moves a proposal forward, but strong opposition can still sink it.

Vote your honest position, ask for evidence, and abstain on papers you haven't read. Direction and design approval aren't promises to ship. A firm minority can block, but once heard, it can't just keep saying no.

Plenary is where objections are counted, not born, so raise yours by 5 p.m. the evening before. Then the countries vote, and the committee draft round is where a good comment can still change the text.

</div>

You can read a room now, and you don't have to follow it. Notice how often the answer is "show me the evidence." That's exactly what a good proposal brings, and chapter 9 shows you how to build one.

## 9. What Goes into a Proposal

You have an idea, and you think it belongs in C++. This chapter is the cold shower and the toolkit: what a proposal must bring to earn the committee's time, and why the bar is so high.

- The difference between getting heard and getting a yes, and the four arguments a real proposal makes

- Writing an abstract a busy stranger can grasp
- Tony Tables, and why honest ones win
- Steel-manning your own idea, twice
- Implementation experience and the feature-test macro
- Writing wording that reads like law, and why your first draft needs a CWG or LWG reader
- Teaching your feature with a short tutorial
- Scope, dependencies, and the high bar

</div>

### 9.1 What Belongs in a Paper

Getting a paper heard is the low bar. A paper, on time, with someone to present it, and the room will look at it. But getting a yes is a different game.

A real proposal makes four arguments. It shows the problem is worth solving, lays out the design, weighs the alternatives, and proves the thing works. Miss one and a reviewer will find the hole. And do your homework: cite the earlier papers on the same ground, because the room remembers them.

You don't need coauthors. The Direction Group's [P4024R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p4024r0.pdf) says a paper from "a single author citing feedback from diverse reviewers" is just as valid as a coauthored one. So thank your reviewers by name.

### 9.2 The Three Pillars

Strong papers share three habits, call them the *three pillars*. They lead with concrete examples, argue from clear principles, and show the alternatives they weighed. A paper missing a pillar feels thin.

Examples do the heavy lifting. Show real before-and-after code, not abstract claims. A reader who sees the improvement needs less convincing than one who's only told about it.

The pillars come from the committee itself. [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) asks for all three and calls them the difference between "someone's cool idea" and "a real proposal."

### 9.3 The Abstract as Elevator Pitch

Most readers decide from the abstract alone. So write it as an elevator pitch a busy stranger can grasp. The first sentence should state your finding, not wind up to it.

Keep it honest, though. Promise only what the paper delivers, because reviewers punish hype. A title that names the result beats a title that names the topic.

### 9.4 Tony Tables

The committee's favorite tool is the *Tony Table*, a side-by-side of old code and new. You put the current way on the left and your way on the right. The reader sees the win without you claiming it.

The name honors Tony Van Eerd, who came up with the format. His [tables of C++17 features](https://github.com/tvaneerd/cpp17_in_TTs) show it at its best.

Make the table honest to earn trust. Include the cases where your design isn't shorter or clearer. A table that hides its weak spots gets caught and loses the room.

### 9.5 Show the Alternatives: The Steel Man

Don't dodge the alternatives. Attack your own idea first. A *steel man* is the strongest possible case against your proposal, stated fairly, which you then answer with evidence.

Build two of them. One argues that no standard feature is needed at all, that a library would do. The other argues that a rival design is better, and then you show why yours wins.

Don't stack the deck. The Direction Group's [P2000R5](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf) says that showing only your design's strengths and only a rival's weaknesses "is not acceptable."

The strongest answer to every objection is the same: it already works.

### 9.6 Implementation Experience

Nothing beats proof that your idea works. *Implementation experience* means you've built the feature and used it, ideally in more than one place. The committee wants that proof before it freezes anything forever.

No rule demands it, though. The [submit-a-proposal page](https://isocpp.org/std/submit-a-proposal) says there's no ISO requirement to build it first, only to win consensus. But it adds that "consensus is easier to achieve when there's a working implementation."

<div id="watch-out">

Not required doesn't mean optional. Show up with no code and you're asking the room to take your word for it, and that's a hard sell. Build your feature anyway, even for your first paper.

</div>

The bar is real but not impossible. For a library, ship code that compiles, passes tests, and has real users. For a language feature, a rough compiler fork that shows it working is enough to start.

One small piece rides along with new features: the *feature-test macro*. It's a predefined name that lets code check whether a compiler has your feature yet. Including it lets people adopt the feature safely while support spreads. The rules and the full list live in [SD-FeatureTest](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-6-sg10-feature-test-recommendations).

### 9.7 Writing Standard Wording

Standard text isn't prose, it's closer to law. Two words do most of the work: *shall* marks a hard requirement, and *should* marks advice. Mixing them up changes what implementers must do.

Point to the right place, too. Cite the stable section labels (the bracketed names from chapter 1), not page or section numbers. The numbers shift between drafts. The labels don't.

Don't write your first wording alone. Ask someone from CWG or LWG to read your draft before you submit, not after the room finds the holes. The chairs will help you find someone.

### 9.8 A Short Tutorial

A great proposal teaches its feature. Include a short tutorial that shows someone how to use it. P2000 asks for one early, because a tutorial keeps a feature meant for "ordinary programmers" from drifting into expert-only territory.

If you can't explain it in a few clear lines, the design may be too complex.

### 9.9 Scope and Dependencies

Keep each paper to one topic. If your title needs the word "and," split it in two. Big, bundled proposals stall, while small focused ones move.

For a large idea, ship it in stages. Land the useful piece first, let people use it, and build the rest on top. Some features even wait on another paper to land before they can.

### 9.10 The High Bar

The burden of proof sits on you, the proposer. The default is no, because a mistake is forever. Being useful isn't enough, so show why only the standard can deliver what you need.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

Be convinced by overwhelming evidence and nothing else. Not by the room, not by a big name, and least of all by how much you love your own idea.

</div>

Language features are the hardest of all. A library lives on GitHub if the committee says no, but a language feature has nowhere else to go. So the committee guards the language even more tightly than the library.

Most papers don't make it, and that's the system working. Big efforts have stalled or died. The [Networking TS](https://www.iso.org/standard/64030.html) came out in 2018 and still isn't in the standard, and the [Transactional Memory TS](https://www.iso.org/standard/66343.html) never merged.

<div id="short-version">

Getting heard is easy. Getting a yes is hard. Build on the three pillars SD-4 asks for: examples, principles, and alternatives. Show an honest Tony Table, answer two steel men, and prove it works. Get a CWG or LWG reader for your wording. Keep to one topic, and expect the default answer to be no.

</div>

Keep loving your idea, but love it with clear eyes. Now let's actually write and submit the paper.

## 10. Producing and Submitting Your Paper

You're ready to write the paper. Here's the practical path: the tools, the format, and the route from your editor to the committee's agenda.

- The tools that format your paper, and the template to start from
- The one document that covers paper numbers, format, and the mailing deadline
- Making the paper easy to read, even on a phone between sessions
- A prototype that readers can run in the browser and study in a public repo
- Coauthors, revision after revision, and the patent rule
- How to get on the agenda, and why asking the chair isn't pushy
- Why design approval is a fifth of the work

</div>

### 10.1 Float the Idea First

Before you write a full paper, float the idea. Post a short description to [std-proposals](https://lists.isocpp.org/mailman/listinfo.cgi/std-proposals) (the public list from chapter 4) and see who bites. The official [submit-a-proposal](https://isocpp.org/std/submit-a-proposal) page walks you through it.

This saves you from writing a paper nobody wants. You'll find prior art, hear objections early, and maybe meet a coauthor. A warm reception here is a green light.

Floating the idea also finds your competitors before the room does. Check for other papers on the same problem, and talk to their authors first. The Direction Group even has a name for this, the "no surprise competitors" rule in [P4024R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p4024r0.pdf).

Best case, you end up writing one paper together. If you can't merge, write a joint paper that lays out the trade-offs. Chairs can ask you to try that before they schedule either paper.

Alone or together, once the idea has interest, you write it up.

### 10.2 Writing the Paper: Tools and Template

You don't format a paper by hand. Most authors use [mpark/wg21](https://github.com/mpark/wg21), which turns Markdown into a committee-styled document, or [Bikeshed](https://github.com/tabatkins/bikeshed), which builds HTML specs. Newer tools like [COWEL](https://github.com/Eisenwave/cowel) and plain LaTeX work too.

Start from the *official template*, the "Template for Library Proposals" at the bottom of the submit-a-proposal page. The tools above ship their own versions. The template gives you the front matter and the expected sections.

### 10.3 The Formatting Standard: SD-7

[SD-7](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-7-mailing-procedures-and-how-to-write-papers) covers the mechanics: how to get a paper number, how to format the document, and how to submit it to a mailing. Read it once before your first paper.

Remember the basics from chapter 3. Your paper gets a P-number and a revision, and it has to land before the mailing deadline. SD-7 puts that deadline at 2 p.m. UTC on the Monday four weeks before the meeting. A late paper waits for the next round.

You upload the paper at [isocpp.org/papers](https://isocpp.org/papers), which needs an isocpp.org login. For a new paper, pick "New Paper Number" and the system assigns your P-number. For a revision, pick your earlier paper, and the system bumps the R for you. Make sure the R on your title page matches.

Two buttons finish the job. "Save Draft" keeps a working copy you can replace as often as you like. "Save and Mark for Review" marks the paper ready for the mailing. That's the click that counts against the deadline.

Once it's in a mailing, your paper gets an issue in the [GitHub tracker](https://github.com/cplusplus/papers) from chapter 5. Watch it to see where you stand.

Before any of that, make sure everyone can read your paper.

### 10.4 Make It Accessible

Use enough color contrast, a monospace font for code, and clear headings. Don't lean on color alone to make a point.

This isn't busywork. A clear paper is one the busy delegate can skim on a phone between sessions. Accessible papers get read.

The strongest part of a paper is proof that it runs.

### 10.5 Prototyping: Compiler Explorer and GitHub

Let readers run your code, don't just describe it. [Compiler Explorer](https://godbolt.org), at godbolt.org, runs C++ in the browser across many compilers. Share a live link and the reader sees it work.

<div id="try-this-today">

Before you write a line of your paper, put your motivating example on Compiler Explorer. Show how people write it now and how your proposal would let them write it, side by side. Save the link. It's often the first thing a reviewer clicks.

</div>

Put your implementation on GitHub. A public repo lets people read the code, file issues, and try it themselves. It also tracks your revisions as the design evolves.

Few papers are solo efforts, and none survive unchanged.

### 10.6 Coauthoring and Revision History

Many papers have more than one author. Coauthors bring expertise, share the load, and lend credibility. List everyone in the front matter, and name one person as the presenter.

Expect to revise, again and again. Each revision (R0, R1, R2) answers the last round of feedback. Reviewers read what changed, so spell out your changes.

Before you present, don't forget one legal step.

### 10.7 Patent Disclosure

ISO has a patent rule, and you must follow it. If you know of a patent that covers your proposal, disclose it before the work advances. The [ISO patent policy](https://www.iso.org/iso-standards-and-patents.html) spells out the duty.

With the paper done and disclosed, you need a slot to present it.

### 10.8 Getting on the Agenda

A paper with no presenter goes nowhere. Email the chair (the person running the room) before the meeting to ask for agenda time. Confirm whether you'll present in person or by video.

<div id="watch-out">

Don't wait to be invited. A paper in the mailing isn't a paper on the agenda. If you don't ask the chair for a slot, you may find out on Monday that yours was never in the plan. Asking isn't pushy. Waiting can cost you a whole meeting.

</div>

When your slot comes, be ready to present and to listen. You'll show the problem, the design, and the proof, then take questions. The room's reaction steers your next revision.

Getting through that first discussion feels like the finish line. It isn't.

### 10.9 The Other 80%

Winning design approval (the "this design is right" gate from chapter 8) feels like victory. It's the first fifth. Wording review and the national ballots are what the committee's own page on [the life of a proposal](https://isocpp.org/std/the-life-of-an-iso-proposal) calls "the other 80%."

After design comes the long part. A wording group polishes the exact text, then the countries vote it through the ballot stages. Plan for years, not months, and don't celebrate too early.

<div id="short-version">

Float the idea on std-proposals first, and talk to anyone working on the same problem. Write it up with mpark/wg21 or Bikeshed, starting from the official template, and follow SD-7 for the number, format, and deadline. Make it easy to read, even on a phone.

Put your code on Compiler Explorer and GitHub so people can run it. Name a presenter, expect to revise again and again, and disclose any patent you know of. Then email the chair for agenda time. Design approval feels like the finish line, but it's a fifth of the work.

</div>

Writing the paper is only the start. Getting people to say yes is the real work, and that's the long art of championing, covered next.

- Disagreement versus opposition, and when to withdraw
- How reputation compounds, and how the presenter is judged
- The long game, the headwinds, and protecting yourself

</div>

### 11.1 Before the Room: Presocialization

Most papers are won before they're presented. *Presocialization* means talking up your idea in hallways and on the lists, building support ahead of the room. A cold presentation to a surprised room usually stalls.

The decisive moment is the five minutes before a poll. The chair's summary, the first voice to speak, and the room's mood shape the result. Line up a few supporters who'll speak early.

Supporters aren't conjured the week before a poll. You earn them over years, starting with other people's papers. Send an author a careful review, then another on the next revision, and before long authors start asking you to look at their drafts. The people whose papers you review tend to review yours.

A careful review is rare, so authors remember who sent one. Keep at it, and they'll ask for more: a section, an implementation, and finally your name on the paper. People you've coauthored with will speak up for your paper in rooms you can't be in. That's where champions come from.

### 11.2 Finding and Being a Champion

Every paper needs a champion (the person who presents and pushes it, from chapter 2). Often that's you, the author, but it doesn't have to be. If you can't attend, find someone who knows the work deeply, not just someone to read it aloud.

You can also champion someone else's paper. Backing a good idea you didn't write builds trust and skill. It's one of the fastest ways to become useful.

### 11.3 The First Gate: "Do We Want It at All?"

The first discussion of your paper is the scary one. The room asks "do we want this at all?" before it touches any detail. A no here kills the idea outright.

Frame your opening as solving a problem the committee already cares about. Tie it to the Direction Group's priorities from chapter 5, laid out in [P5000R1](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p5000r1.pdf). A paper that matches the agenda gets heard. One that doesn't waits.

Even a wanted paper will take fire.

### 11.4 When the Poll Goes Against You

A poll against you is not a death sentence. Being sent back for more work is the normal path, not a rejection. You take the feedback, revise, and return with a stronger paper.

Know the one risky move: reopening a settled question. *Re-litigation* is re-debating something the room already decided, and it costs you credibility. Reopen only with genuinely new evidence, not the same argument again.

When you're stuck, the way forward is often a smaller ask.

### 11.5 Negotiating: Back-Pocket and Max-Min

Always have a fallback. A *back-pocket alternative* is a second design you're ready to offer if your first choice is rejected. Walking in with one keeps you from leaving empty-handed.

When the room is split, shrink the ask. The *max-min solution* is the smallest version everyone can accept. A small win that ships beats a big proposal that stalls.

### 11.6 Disagreement versus Opposition, and When to Withdraw

Learn to tell two things apart. Disagreement is a technical objection, usually with a paper behind it. Opposition is a person set against your idea, and the two need different responses.

Sometimes the right move is to stop. If the room clearly doesn't want it and won't budge, withdraw the paper with grace.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

Knowing when to stop is a skill, not a defeat. A dead paper keeps eating the time and credibility your next one will need.

</div>

### 11.7 How Reputation Works

In a room of volunteers, reputation is what gets your paper read. People can't review every proposal, so they weigh who wrote it. A trusted name gets the benefit of the doubt, while a new one gets extra scrutiny.

You build it in small steps. Start with modest, useful contributions, become the reliable expert in one narrow area, and use your credibility only on the battles that matter. Reputation compounds when you're right over time.

### 11.8 The Presenter Is Judged Too

The committee weighs the presenter, not only the proposal. Are you reasonable, willing to concede a fair point, someone people want to work with? Those signals shape how your paper lands.

Don't be the obstacle. Causing needless delay, or escalating every dispute, marks you as hard to work with. The escalation path from chapter 8 exists, but [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) warns that using it topic after topic erodes your credibility.

Persistence does pay, though. *Procedural momentum* is the benefit a paper earns from many revisions and prior polls. By the fourth or fifth revision, the room tends to assume it'll pass, so steady iteration works in your favor.

### 11.9 The Long Game

Set your clock to years, not months. The train runs every three years, and most features ride more than one. Patience isn't optional here. It's the job.

The famous features all took ages. Coroutines, modules, and the executors work that became [`std::execution`](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2024/p2300r10.html) each ran many years and many revisions. Stackful coroutines have waited over a decade. Their paper got to [revision 24](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p0876r24.pdf) in July 2026, and they still aren't in.

### 11.10 The Structural Headwinds

Some headwinds aren't about you. The first is the *Bandwidth Gap*: far more papers arrive than anyone can review. Counting revisions, the committee puts out eight hundred or more papers a year, and the median delegate reads a few dozen.

<div id="watch-out">

A quiet inbox isn't a *no*. When nobody writes back about your paper, most people simply haven't read it yet. Give up over that, and you've lost to a crowded mailing, not to a bad idea.

</div>

Another is the *expert bubble*. Papers are often written by experts for experts, which makes it hard for newcomers and outside ideas to break in. The review system is one crack in that wall, so use it.

Resources tilt the field, too. Proposals with paid committee time and funded implementations move faster than volunteer efforts. It's not a conspiracy, just the weight of who can afford to show up every cycle.

Those forces wear people down, and that brings us to the last thing worth protecting.

### 11.11 The Emotional Side

Having your paper picked apart stings, and that's normal. Don't take it personally. The room argues hard about ideas, not about you.

Burnout is real here, and feeling it isn't a weakness. In a [2020 pulse poll](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2020/p2260r0.pdf), over 60 percent of those who answered said they often felt exhausted by meetings and other demands. Good people leave because of it. Set boundaries, pace yourself, and know when to step back.

Protecting your energy is protecting your impact. The committee runs for decades, and it needs you for the long haul, not for one heroic burst. Pace beats sprint.

<div id="short-version">

Win your paper in the hallway before the room, and earn supporters years earlier by reviewing, then coauthoring, other people's papers. Find a champion or be one. Frame your idea around the Direction Group's priorities. A poll against you is feedback, not a death sentence, but don't re-litigate without new evidence.

Keep a back-pocket alternative ready, and shrink to the max-min solution when the room splits. Tell disagreement from opposition, and know when to withdraw. Reputation compounds, and so does procedural momentum. Think in years, mind the Bandwidth Gap and the expert bubble, and pace yourself.

</div>

Now you can champion a paper and survive the years it takes. Some proposals die for reasons no campaign can fix. Next, the deep design rules that decide what's even possible.

## 12. C++ Design Principles

By now you've seen good ideas stall. The reason often isn't politics, it's a handful of deep design rules that decide what C++ can even accept. This chapter names them.

<div id="in-this-chapter">

- Where the rules come from, and the book to read before you propose a language change
- Why the safest path is to standardize what already exists
- Backward compatibility, and why the committee rarely ships two versions of a type
- The zero-overhead principle
- ABI, the constraint you're least likely to see coming, and where the committee stands on breaking it
- Freestanding and hosted environments, the two worlds C++ runs in

</div>

Most of these rules aren't new. Bjarne Stroustrup wrote them down in 1994 in [*The Design and Evolution of C++*](https://www.stroustrup.com/dne.html), the book everyone calls D&E. It explains why each early feature is the way it is, and what was tried and thrown out.

Read it before you write a language proposal. Many of the objections you'll hear are in it, with the answers.

### 12.1 Standardize Existing Practice

The committee's safest path is to *standardize existing practice*. That means blessing a design that already works in the field, instead of inventing something new at the table. A design with years of real use behind it clears the bar far faster.

The pattern shows up again and again. The [fmt](https://github.com/fmtlib/fmt) library became `std::format`, and [range-v3](https://github.com/ericniebler/range-v3) became `std::ranges`, each after proving itself in the wild. Designs invented at the table are a gamble.

Why so cautious? Because the standard can't easily fix its mistakes.

### 12.2 Backward Compatibility

C++ keeps a hard promise: old code keeps working. This is *backward compatibility*, and it guards decades of existing programs. A change that breaks working code faces enormous resistance.

This is also why the committee rarely ships two versions of the same type. A second, "safer" vector would split the language, breaking code that passes one kind where the other is expected. So one type has to serve everyone.

When the committee does add a second type, everyone lives with both. C++20 put `std::jthread` beside `std::thread` rather than change the old one. Now you have two thread types and have to pick.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

The promise that old code keeps working blocks some good changes, maybe yours. It also protects all the code you've already written. Respect it on the days it says no.

</div>

### 12.3 The Zero-Overhead Principle

C++ lives by the *zero-overhead principle*. It has two halves: you don't pay for what you don't use, and what you do use runs as fast as hand-written code. A feature that taxes people who never touch it tends to fail.

This shapes every proposal. If your feature slows down code that doesn't use it, expect to defend that cost in writing. The committee protects the people who came for speed.

### 12.4 ABI: The Invisible Constraint

The constraint you're least likely to see coming is *ABI*, the application binary interface. ABI is the binary contract between compiled pieces of a program: the memory layout of types and the way functions are called. Change it, and programs already built stop working until they're rebuilt.

This is why some slow types never get faster. Speeding up `std::regex` would change its layout and break every binary that uses it. The hash map `std::unordered_map` is stuck twice over, because its own interface demands the slow design. So the committee keeps the slow versions, and the market routes around them.

The committee took this on at the *Prague ABI vote* in early 2020, arguing over two papers by Titus Winters. [P1863R1](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2020/p1863r1.pdf) was "ABI - Now or Never," and [P2028R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2020/p2028r0.pdf) asked "What is ABI, and What Should WG21 Do About It?"

The committee chose not to break ABI across the library for C++23, while refusing to promise stability forever. In practice, that left existing binaries safe and the frozen types frozen.

The ABI Review Group now advises on proposals that touch layout or calling conventions.

<div id="watch-out">

If your proposal changes the layout of a type people already use, expect the ABI question. Answer it in your paper, where you have time to think. On your feet in the room, the same question is much harder.

</div>

### 12.5 Freestanding versus Hosted

C++ runs in two very different worlds, and the standard names them. A *hosted* environment has an operating system and the full library behind it. A *freestanding* environment has neither, like a microcontroller, a kernel, or a bootloader.

This split shapes what a library feature can assume. Something that needs files or threads usually won't work freestanding, since there may be no operating system to provide them. A proposal that forgets the freestanding world will hear about it from the people who live there.

<div id="short-version">

Most of these rules come from Stroustrup's D&E, so read it before you write a language proposal. Your safest bet is a design that already works in the field. Old code must keep working, so one type has to serve everyone. When a second type does ship, like `std::jthread`, everyone lives with both.

Don't make people pay for a feature they don't use. Watch for ABI, since changing a type's layout breaks programs already built. In 2020 the committee chose not to break it for C++23, without promising it never will. And remember the freestanding world, where there may be no operating system at all.

</div>

These constraints explain the quiet noes: an idea can be right and still impossible. Knowing them saves you from proposing the unbuildable.

## 13. Common Mistakes

You've made it far enough to be dangerous. This chapter lists the traps that catch newcomers, each with its fix. Read it once now, and again before your first meeting.

<div id="in-this-chapter">

- Showing up: why you announce yourself, and what changes after your first meeting
- Your first paper: why core language can wait, what evidence to bring, and who else is working on the problem
- In the room: when to abstain, what to keep private, when to object, how consensus works, and how much to talk
- After the poll: why encouragement isn't approval, why you follow subgroup direction, and why design approval isn't the finish line

</div>

### 13.1 Showing Up Without Announcing

The first trap is simply appearing. The convener controls access, so guests give about a week's notice and members register ahead. Email the convener before you go, and catch the Sunday orientation.

The same trap waits a meeting later. Guest status covers your first meeting only. Before your second, talk to a national body about joining, so your name gets into the global directory.

### 13.2 Proposing Core Language Features Too Early

Bringing a language feature before you know the room is a hard road. Core changes face the highest bar of all (see chapter 9), and a cold first attempt usually fails. Watch how the room works first, socialize the idea, and bring evidence.

### 13.3 Writing an Idea-Only Paper

An idea with no examples, alternatives, or proof wastes the room's time. The bar is cultural, not enforced, but thin papers still go nowhere. Bring worked examples and an implementation, even on your first paper (see chapter 9).

### 13.4 Skipping the Search for Competing Papers

Someone may already be working on your problem. If you don't check, you've set up a fight you didn't need. Talk first. The Direction Group asks for "no surprise competitors" in [P4024R0](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p4024r0.pdf). Search the mailings, write to the other authors, and say in your paper that you did.

### 13.5 Voting on Things You Haven't Followed

Voting with the room on a paper you didn't read is a quiet mistake. Your passive vote still counts under consensus, so it muddies the signal. When you haven't followed a topic, abstain (the honest non-vote from chapter 8) instead of guessing.

And don't hide in neutral. Neutral means you read the paper and truly land in the middle.

### 13.6 Quoting Reflectors or Notes Publicly

Pasting a reflector post or meeting note into a blog breaks the rules. Those are private under the confidentiality rule, and quoting them publicly is a real breach. Paraphrase afterward instead, and share only poll questions and numbers freely. Never post from inside a session, even in your own words.

### 13.7 Raising Concerns Too Late

Saving your objection for the closing plenary gains nothing. Concerns belong in the subgroup, early, where the work happens. Raise it in the room, bring a paper, tell your national body, and beat the 5 p.m. deadline the evening before plenary.

### 13.8 Expecting Majority Rule

Counting hands and expecting the bigger number to win is wrong here. The committee runs on consensus, the absence of sustained opposition, not majority rule.

A determined minority can stall a paper, and a bare majority can't simply outvote it. But being heard isn't a veto. Once objectors have made their case, they can't just keep saying no.

### 13.9 Talking Too Much Too Soon

Speaking constantly at your first meetings works against you. Newcomers build trust by learning the room before filling it with their voice. Listen first, contribute something specific, and let your work speak.

### 13.10 Misreading Encouragement as Approval

Hearing "come back with more" and reading it as "this will ship" sets you up for a fall. An early warm poll is direction to keep going, not adoption (the encouragement-is-not-approval point from chapter 8). Real approval comes later, at plenary, after wording.

### 13.11 Ignoring Subgroup Direction

Getting direction from a room and then ignoring it stalls your paper. The design rooms exist to set direction, and working against it keeps the opposition alive. Follow the guidance, or reopen it in that same room with a paper, but don't route around it.

### 13.12 Declaring Victory at Design Approval

Treating design approval as the finish line is the last big trap. It's an early milestone, with wording review and the national ballots still ahead (the "other 80%" from chapter 10). Budget for the long tail, and don't celebrate until it ships.

<div id="short-version">

Email the convener before you go, and talk to a national body before your second meeting. Learn the room before you propose core language features, and give every paper worked examples and an implementation. Search for competing papers and write to their authors.

Abstain on anything you haven't followed. Paraphrase instead of quoting reflectors or notes, and never post from inside a session. Raise objections early in the subgroup, and tell your national body. Expect consensus, not majority rule. Listen first and let your work speak.

Treat a warm poll as encouragement, not approval. Follow subgroup direction, or reopen it there with a paper. Design approval is a start, so budget for wording review and the ballots.

</div>

Last, don't treat this notebook as scripture. The committee's rules and habits keep changing, sometimes from one meeting to the next, so parts of it will age. Read each new [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) and each trip report, and trust them over anything here.

One mistake underlies them all: confusing being busy with making a difference. Showing up and passing polls can feel like impact, but a feature that never ships helped nobody. Keep your eyes on the people who use C++.

That's the end of the notebook, and the start of your part in it. Keep your wits about you, demand evidence, and put the users first. Now go do it well.
