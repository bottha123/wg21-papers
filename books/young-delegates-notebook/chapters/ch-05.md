## 5. How WG21 Is Structured

WG21 looks like a wall of acronyms at first. EWG, LEWG, and fifteen study groups blur together. This chapter turns that wall into a clear map.

### 5.1 One Committee, Many Rooms

WG21 does its work in several groups people call *the rooms*. Each room owns one slice of the job. They meet at the same time, in parallel, all week.

That parallel timetable is the *schedule grid*. Six or seven rooms run at once, so you can't attend them all. You pick one room and follow it.

The rooms split into two jobs. Some decide what to build (design), and others write the exact words (wording).

### 5.2 The Evolution Rooms: EWG and LEWG

Two rooms decide whether a feature is worth building. *EWG*, the Evolution Working Group, handles the core language. It judges proposed language features.

*LEWG*, the Library Evolution Working Group, does the same for the standard library. It decides which library features the committee wants. Both rooms answer the design question, not the wording one.

### 5.3 The Wording Rooms: CWG and LWG

Two more rooms turn approved designs into exact standard text. *CWG*, the Core Working Group, reviews and polishes the language wording. It hunts for gaps and ambiguity in the spec.

*LWG*, the Library Working Group, does the same for the library. It's careful, detailed work on the precise words. These rooms ask whether the text says what the design rooms approved, not whether the feature is wanted.

### 5.4 Study Groups

New ideas usually start in a *study group*, or SG. A study group focuses on one area, like concurrency or networking, and shapes raw ideas.

They're numbered SG1 through SG23. Fifteen are active as of 2026, and the rest sit dormant until new papers wake them up. The [committee page](https://isocpp.org/std/the-committee) lists them all.

Each study group is a world of its own, deep in its specialty. When an idea is ready, it graduates to a design room. The convener (the person who runs WG21) creates and closes study groups as needs change.

### 5.5 How a Paper Moves Between Groups

A paper passes through a chain of groups. A study group shapes it, a design room approves it, a wording room finalizes it, and the plenary (the full committee) adopts it. Each handoff is a vote to send it onward.

Some papers touch more than one room. A feature that changes the language and the library visits both evolution rooms, sometimes together in a joint session. The groups coordinate so nothing falls through the cracks.

### 5.6 The Train Model

C++ ships on a fixed schedule, and that rule is *the train model*. Every three years a new standard leaves the station with whatever features are ready. C++14, 17, 20, 23, and 26 all rode it.

Herb Sutter proposed it in 2011 in [N3316](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2011/n3316.pdf). Before it, C++11 slipped for years. It's been the model since 2012, and [P1000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p1000r7.pdf) lays out the current timetable. The schedule traded "now or never" for "catch the next train."

The model has a safety valve: pull what isn't ready. The committee did exactly that in 2019 with [P1823](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2019/p1823r0.pdf), taking contracts out of C++20 to let them bake longer. The valve works, but pulling a big feature is never cheap.

### 5.7 How Features Get Removed

Taking a feature out is far harder than putting one in. Once a feature ships, real programs depend on it, and breaking them isn't an option. So the standard almost only grows.

There's a difference between pulling and removing. Pulling takes a feature out before it ships, which happens now and then. Removing something already shipped is rare, and it can take many years.

### 5.8 The People Who Run It

A handful of officers keep the committee running. The convener appoints the chairs, creates study groups, sets the schedule, and judges whether the full committee agrees. [SC22](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/), WG21's parent committee, appoints the convener for terms of up to three years, with no limit on renewals. Two vice-conveners and a secretary help run things.

Two *project editors* keep the working draft. They're the people who apply every approved change to the text of the standard. It's quiet, exacting work that holds the whole document together.

Each room has its own small crew. A *chair* runs the session, and someone takes the minutes. Anyone in the room can be asked to scribe, so it might be you.

### 5.9 Who Sets Priorities: The Direction Group

A small senior group, the Direction Group from chapter 3, steers the committee's focus. It writes down where C++ should go in [P2000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf) and what the next standard should put first in [P5000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p5000r1.pdf). Membership is by invitation, not election.

The Direction Group doesn't decide individual papers. It sets the big-picture priorities that chairs lean on when scheduling. Framing your work to match those priorities helps it get attention.

### 5.10 Chair Power

The chair of a room holds more power than it looks. The chair decides which papers make the agenda, when they come up, how the polls are worded, and when the room has agreed. None of that is a vote, yet all of it shapes outcomes.

The strongest power is the calendar. A chair who never schedules a paper can stall it for years, without ever voting against it. So getting on the agenda is its own battle.

The calendar does have rules, though. [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures) tells chairs what comes first: work tied to the Direction Group's priorities, work aimed at the next standard, and work that can make progress at this meeting. Everything else gets time as time allows.

You can watch the queue yourself. Every paper gets a tracking issue in a public list on [GitHub](https://github.com/cplusplus/papers). It shows you what's waiting and what's moving.

### 5.11 The Rules: Standing Documents

The committee's own rules live in [standing documents](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents) (the numbered SD files from chapter 3). Eight are current, and the numbering has gaps because older ones were retired. They run from study-group setup to how to write papers. The main one is SD-4, the practices and procedures.

Standing documents change slowly and carefully. New ones need the full committee's agreement. A standing document only goes away when someone retires it, so old practices tend to stay.

### 5.12 The Limits of Power: Volunteers and the Implementer Veto

Here's the truth under all the structure: nobody can be ordered to do anything. WG21 is volunteers, and it only makes recommendations.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

A proposal lives only as long as someone keeps showing up for it. Nobody can be assigned to do that for you. If it's your idea, the someone is you.

</div>

The compiler teams hold a quiet *implementer veto*. If GCC, Clang, and MSVC (the three implementers from chapter 1) won't build a feature, the standard can't make them.

It's a veto over what you can use, not over what gets adopted. The committee can standardize a feature nobody has built yet. Then the standard says one thing, your compiler does another, and you live in the gap.

This isn't only theory. In January 2026, eighteen implementers wrote [P3962](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p3962r0.pdf) asking the committee to slow down, because features pile up faster than they can build them. The people who turn the standard real are stretched thin.

EWG and LEWG decide what to build, CWG and LWG write the exact words, study groups feed them, and the plenary adopts. The train model ships C++ every three years with whatever's ready, and shipped features almost never come back out.

The convener runs the committee, the Direction Group sets priorities, and the chairs control the calendar. The rules live in eight standing documents, SD-4 above all. Nobody can be ordered to do anything. The compiler teams can refuse to build a feature. That's a veto over what you can use, not over what gets adopted.
