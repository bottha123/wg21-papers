## 3. How Papers Work

All the committee's work flows through one kind of object: the paper. Learn how papers work, and the rest of the process makes sense.

### 3.1 Nothing Happens Without a Paper

One rule explains most of the committee: no paper, no proposal. The rulebook, [SD-4](https://isocpp.org/std/standing-documents/sd-4-wg21-practices-and-procedures), puts it bluntly: "if a proposal doesn't have a paper, it doesn't exist." A *paper* is a written proposal or report, and it's the unit of all the work.

A paper needs three things before a room can act on it. The paper has to exist, it has to arrive on time, and someone has to be there to present it. Miss one and the room moves on.

Talk still matters. A hallway conversation can expose a flaw, improve a design, or change a mind. But the room only votes on what it can read, so if you want to move the committee, write it down.

### 3.2 What a Paper Is and Its Types

Papers sort first by what they want from you. An *ask-paper* proposes something and asks for a poll. An *inform-paper* puts facts on the record and asks for nothing. Those aren't official labels, just useful ones.

Here's a quiet truth: the inform-paper often gets read more. It asks for no time on a crowded schedule. So it slips in where an ask-paper waits in line.

Papers also split by job. Some argue for a design, and others nail down exact standard wording. A separate kind, the *standing document*, holds the committee's own rules instead of proposing anything.

Whatever its kind, every paper goes by a number, and the number itself tells you a lot.

### 3.3 The P-Number System

A paper's number starts with a letter that tells you what sort of document it is. A *P-number* marks a proposal, like [P2300](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2024/p2300r10.html). A *D-number* marks a draft that hasn't been published yet.

An *N-number* marks an official ISO document, like the working draft itself, an agenda, or minutes. Before 2015 every paper got one, so an older N-number is often a proposal.

After the number comes a *revision*, like R0 for the first version, then R1, R2, and up. Read the newest one you can find.

R0 is brand new and untested in the room. A paper at R5 has been reworked five times.

<div id="rule-of-thumb">

A paper's revision number tells you its history, not its quality. A fifth revision means someone kept working, not that they got it right. Judge what's on the page.

</div>

Once you have a number, the paper is one link away.

### 3.4 Finding Any Paper

You don't hunt for papers by hand. Three sites will find you any paper, but only one is the real archive. That's [open-std.org](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/), where every paper lives in a plain list by date. Every paper link in this notebook points there.

A friendlier view of the same papers is [wg21.org](https://wg21.org). It's searchable and sorted by group, and each entry links back to the original on open-std.org. Treat it as a window onto the archive, not the archive itself.

The quickest route is wg21.link, an unofficial shortcut that Mara Bos has run for years. Type a paper number after the slash, in lowercase, and it sends you to the copy on open-std.org. For P2300 revision 10, that's `wg21.link/p2300r10`.

### 3.5 The Mailing System and Deadlines

Papers arrive in batches called *the mailing*, most months of the year. The biggest ones land just before and just after each meeting. In 2025 there were nine in all. Counting revisions, that's eight hundred or more papers a year.

Timing decides what the committee can act on. Each meeting has a *pre-meeting mailing deadline*, weeks ahead of it. Miss it and your paper sits out until the next round.

From that first mailing, a paper begins a long journey.

### 3.6 The Paper Lifecycle

A paper that makes it follows a set route called *the paper lifecycle*. It starts as an idea and ends in the published standard. Most papers stop somewhere along the way.

The ones that finish pass through the same stages, in the same order:

1. You submit the paper to a mailing.
2. A small group incubates the idea and shapes it.
3. A design group decides whether the direction is right.
4. A wording group turns the approved design into exact standard text.
5. The plenary, the full committee, votes it into the working draft (the living text that becomes the next standard).
6. The national bodies review the whole draft and send comments, and the committee answers every one. [C++26 drew 411 of them](https://herbsutter.com/2026/03/29/c26-is-done-trip-report-march-2026-iso-c-standards-meeting-london-croydon-uk/).
7. The national bodies vote to approve it, and ISO publishes the new standard.

Any stage can be the end. A paper can stall, get sent back, or quietly die partway through. The whole journey takes years.

The committee keeps a longer, [stage-by-stage version](https://isocpp.org/std/the-life-of-an-iso-proposal) of this path, and it's worth reading once.

### 3.7 Design Review versus Wording Review

Two of those stages, design and wording, ask very different questions. *Design review* asks: is this the right feature, built the right way? *Wording review* asks: is the exact text precise and complete?

These take different mindsets. Design review argues over whether C++ should have the feature and what shape it should take. Wording review assumes the answer is yes and hunts for gaps in the spec text.

The two happen in order. First a design group approves the direction. Then a wording group takes over and writes the standard text.

Not every paper proposes something new. Some just fix mistakes.

### 3.8 Defect Reports and the Issues Lists

The standard ships with bugs like anything else. Anyone can report one as an *issue*, and the committee decides whether it needs a fix. Once the full committee accepts a fix, the issue becomes a *defect report*, a wording repair rather than a new feature.

These bugs are tracked in public on two *issues lists*, one for the [core language](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/cwg_active.html) and one for the [library](https://cplusplus.github.io/LWG/lwg-active.html). The wording groups work through them, and the fixes the plenary approves go into the working draft.

### 3.9 Reading a Paper Critically

You can't read eight hundred papers a year, so don't try. Read the ones in your subgroup (the smaller group you follow) and skim the rest. Read deeply only where you'll vote.

Always read the newest revision. An old one makes you raise a concern the author already fixed. When a paper comes back, compare it against the last version to see what changed.

Before you form an opinion on any paper, run it through a few questions:

- What problem does it solve, and does it say so?
- Does it weigh other options, or pretend none exist?
- Has anyone built it, or is it only an idea?
- What old code breaks, and how do users move forward?
- Is the wording clear on the hard cases?

The gaps are the red flags. Empty answers, like no implementation and no migration plan, are objections waiting on the floor. A strong paper closes them before you ask. If one doesn't, tell the author.

<div id="try-this-today">

Open the newest mailing on open-std.org and pick one paper on something you use every day. Ask what it fixes, what it breaks, and whether anyone has built it. Write down one gap. That's how every review starts.

</div>

Two documents will save you more than any single paper.

### 3.10 The Documents Everyone Should Read: SD-4 and P2000

Before almost anything else, read the rulebook and the roadmap. The rulebook is SD-4, "WG21 Practices and Procedures," the practices everyone is expected to know. It covers polls, consensus, deadlines, ballots, and how to escalate a disagreement.

The roadmap is [P2000](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2026/p2000r5.pdf), "Direction for ISO C++." A small group of experienced members called the *Direction Group* writes it to recommend where the committee should head over the long run. Reading it tells you which way the wind is blowing.

No paper, no proposal. Every paper lives on open-std.org, and its number tells you its kind and history. New papers arrive in mailings, and a late one sits out a meeting. Most papers never finish the years-long journey to the standard. Read the newest revision critically. And before almost anything else, read SD-4 and P2000.

</div>

All the committee's work flows through papers, and now you know how to read one. Sooner or later you'll want to say something about what you've read, and that's where taking part begins.
